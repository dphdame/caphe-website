Controlling for Post-Start Clinic Visits Subtracts the Care Step That Reduces Hospital Stays

Separating pre-start causes from post-start care steps, sample filters, and Stage 1 checks in health models.

In practice today: Program studies often adjust for all patient, clinic, or county columns at once.[3] Yet adjusting for care steps after start can subtract the service that improves health.[1][3]

What the comparison shows: In care worker trials, clinic visits rose from 57.1% to 71.7% alongside 30% fewer hospital stays.[1] Meanwhile, pre-start poverty explains 62.2% of Santa Clara food-need gaps, and keeping only 55,018 billers in 2024 drops 86 of 95 later bans.[1][2]

Policy option: Reviewers can check when each factor is measured before adding it to a model.[3] Models can adjust for pre-start traits like poverty and leave post-start visits out of total-effect tests.[1][3]

• How Adjusting for Later Clinic Visits Changes the Estimate. In this model, X means program entry, M means primary care visits, and Y means hospital stays. The total effect combines the direct effect with any effect through visits (τ_total = β_direct + α·γ). Adjusting for later visits M removes α·γ. Trials show CHWs raised primary care visits (71.7% vs. 57.1%, odds ratio 1.90) and cut hospital stays by 30%, though the exact share caused by visits requires added assumptions to measure.

• Separate Data Sets and Common-Scale Estimate. Each column uses its own CAPHE study rather than one shared model. Across 408 Santa Clara County tracts, adjusting for poverty and putting measures on a common scale gives a store-distance estimate of -0.0026 (p = 0.827; -0.578 unadjusted and -0.017 in original units).

• Past Billers vs. 2024 Billers. Across all 487,479 billers, the fraud test's AUC is 0.724 (95% range 0.667-0.777), compared with 0.653 after stop-date reweighting and 0.442 (95% range 0.212-0.664) among 55,018 providers still billing in 2024 (9 bans vs. 55,009 comparison billers; months billed ranked at the 38.0th vs. 50.0th peer percentile).

• Checks on Stage 1 Predictors. Across 57 counties in 2003-2016, Lewbel's predictors created from differences in data spread get a first-step F-statistic of 111.20. That F-score tests Stage 1 prediction strength, not the untestable assumption that predictors affect deaths only through budgets. Hansen J gives p = 0.2132, and the check estimates 5.31 fewer deaths per 100,000 per $10 beside 2.38 yearly and 4.32 over 13 years (adjusted p = 0.067).

• Data Sources. Results come from CAPHE Santa Clara tract tables, CHW trial summaries, Medicaid fraud test outputs, and LHA county spending files.

Why Factor Timing Comes Before Choosing Model Controls

Putting all columns into one model treats pre-start causes like care steps after start. A shared cause like neighborhood poverty exists before the factors it may affect. Across 408 Santa Clara tracts, poverty accounts for 62.2% of food-need score differences. Before poverty adjustment, store distance is -0.578, vs. -0.017 after poverty adjustment. By contrast, clinic visits in CHW trials rise from 57.1% to 71.7% (odds ratio 1.90) alongside 30% fewer hospital stays.

Checking when each factor occurs keeps every column in its proper role. Analysts adjust for pre-start causes and do not adjust for later care steps when estimating total effects. Audit teams likewise show full-list fraud scores (0.724; 0.653 reweighted) beside 2024 billers (0.442 across 9 of 95 bans). County checks keep budget predictors in Stage 1 (first-step F = 111.20), not in outcome controls. If predictors affect deaths only through budgets, that check gives 5.31 fewer deaths per 100,000 beside 4.32 (adjusted p = 0.067).

Common Questions and Study Notes

Why can adjusting for post-start clinic visits understate CHW hospital drops?
More clinic visits (rising from 57.1% to 71.7%, odds ratio 1.90) may help explain 30% fewer hospital stays. The trial did not measure that share alone. Adjusting for later visits aims to test effects apart from clinic care and needs added assumptions. So adjusting for visits can remove hospital drops that happen through primary care.
How can an analyst tell a baseline confounder from a post-start care step?
Check whether the factor existed before start or changed after the program began. A baseline confounder like pre-start poverty or age exists first and affects both entry and health. Adjusting for it can cut bias when model rules hold. By contrast, a care step changes after start and may cause part of the gain.
Why is the budget predictor used in the first step of the two-step model?
Two-step models assume the Stage 1 predictor affects death rates only through county spending. Adding it as an outcome control instead adjusts for budget changes that Stage 1 uses. Here, first-step F = 111.20 tests predictor strength, not whether that untestable causal rule holds. Because that rule cannot be tested directly, 5.31 stays an extra check beside 4.32 (adjusted p = 0.067).
