Testing Fraud Checks Only on Current Billers Leaves Out Banned Providers Who Stopped Early

Comparing full-list fraud scores with 2024 billers and adjusting alert rates to all billers.

In practice today: Audit teams score Medicaid billers to flag cases for review, often testing models on current billers or 1-in-5 comparison samples.[1][3] Both choices change what test scores and alert rates mean in practice.[2][3]

What the comparison shows: Across all 487,479 billers, the model scores 0.724 (0.653 reweighted), yet drops to 0.442 on 55,018 billers active in 2024 because 90.5% of later-banned billers stopped early.[1][2] Adjusting the 1-in-5 sample's 0.922% top-1% alert rate to the full list gives 0.186% (9 bans in 4,844 alerts; 9.53x lift).[3]

Policy option: Audit teams can adjust sample alert rates to the 0.0195% full-list rate before sizing review staff.[3] Vendor reviews can also compare full-list scores beside active-biller scores.[1][2]

• How Cases Are Defined. Positive cases are federal OIG LEIE exclusions under the fraud-related laws in the study's Specification A (Social Security Act sections 1128(a)(1), 1128(a)(3), and 1128(b)(1)). Testing earlier claims against later bans compares 95 providers banned in 2025 or later with 487,384 comparison billers (487,479 total).

• Adjusting Alert Rates With Bayes' Rule. The separate test sample keeps all 95 later-banned billers and 20% of comparison billers (97,476 of 487,384). In the full list, comparison billers are 5 times as numerous, so full-list odds equal sample odds divided by 5 (Odds_pop = Odds_holdout / 5). For the top 1%, the 0.922% sample rate (odds 0.009306) divides by 5 to give 0.186% on the full list (lift = 0.1858% / 0.0195% = 9.53x). For the top 5%, 0.512% adjusts to 0.103% (5.28x lift).

• 2024 Biller Group and Billing Stop Dates. In the full file, 90.5% of later-banned billers stopped billing before 2024, compared with 43.6% of comparison billers (38.0th vs. 50.0th peer percentile for months billed). Shorter billing length links strongly with higher model scores (SHAP correlation r = -0.9009, describing model predictions rather than cause). Reweighting comparison billers by stop year gives AUC = 0.653, and testing the 55,018 billers active in 2024 (9 bans and 55,009 comparison billers) gives AUC = 0.442 (95% range 0.212-0.664). These checks show how choosing 2024 billers changes which providers are tested, without claiming stop dates alone explain the 9-case score.

• Active MFCU Cases and Average Cost. National Medicaid Fraud Control Units averaged $23,719 per active case ($395.8M across 16,688 active cases in FY 2024). Applying that active-case average to 4,844 top 1% alerts gives an illustrative $114.9M comparison (not a measured cost of reviewing one alert), while 24,295 top 5% alerts equal 145.6% of active cases.

• Data Sources. Scores, alert counts, and cost comparisons come from the Medicaid fraud model study outputs and HHS-OIG FY 2024 MFCU tables.

Why Fraud Tests Must Account for Billing Stop Dates and Sample Rates

Choosing billers by later billing status can bias a test when past bills and inquiries shape stops. Researchers call this selection, or collider, bias. Across 487,479 billers, 90.5% of billers banned in 2025+ stopped before 2024, vs. 43.6% of comparison billers. The model uses billing length, which may help explain the 0.724 score (0.653 reweighted). Testing only 55,018 billers active in 2024 leaves out 86 stopped bans and keeps 9 of 95, scoring 0.442 (0.50 is chance).

Testing alert rates on a 1-in-5 sample creates a second planning error. Keeping 95 banned billers with 1 in 5 comparison billers cuts false alerts and gives a 0.922% sample rate. Adjusted to the full list's 0.0195% ban rate across 487,479 billers, the top 1% rate is 0.186% (9 bans in 4,844 alerts). Fraud units had 16,688 active cases averaging $23,719 per active case, though single-alert costs were not measured. Showing full-list, reweighted, and 2024 scores with adjusted rates keeps plans clear.

Common Questions and Study Notes

How does filtering to 2024 billers differ from adjusting for a pre-program factor?
A pre-program factor like poverty exists before care starts and affects both program entry and health. Adjusting for it can cut bias when model rules hold. By contrast, still billing in 2024 comes later and reflects past bills and inquiries. Keeping only 2024 billers leaves out 90.5% of later-banned billers who stopped early and changes the test group.
Why do 2024 billers score 0.442 while the full 487,479 list scores 0.724?
On all 487,479 billers, the model uses short billing spans and scores 0.724. Because 90.5% of later-banned billers stopped before 2024 (38.0th vs. 50.0th rank for months billed), stop-date reweighting gives 0.653. Keeping only 55,018 billers in 2024 leaves 9 bans and a 0.442 score, with no clear ranking above chance.
Why must correct alert rates from a 1-in-5 sample be adjusted before planning reviews?
The correct alert rate is the share of alerts matching the formal bans studied here. That share depends on how rare bans are. Keeping 95 banned billers with 1 in 5 comparison billers cuts false alerts and gives a 0.922% sample rate. Adjusting to the full list's 0.0195% rate gives 0.186% (9 bans in 4,844 alerts).
