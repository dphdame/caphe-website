Tract Poverty Explains Most of Santa Clara's Food-Need Score Compared With Store Miles

Comparing how tract poverty and SNAP store miles relate to food-need scores in four counties.

In practice today: County food maps often sort census tracts by miles to the nearest grocery store alone.[1][3] Yet hillside zoning creates long drives in high-pay suburbs while low-pay city renters live near stores.[1][2]

What California records show: In a score model across 408 Santa Clara tracts, poverty explains 62.2% of food-need score gaps while store miles add 0.003 pp (p = 0.827).[1][2] Of 27 tracts past 1 mile, 26 have under 20% poverty, while USDA rules cover 30 of 225 Fresno tracts (up to 15.53 miles).[1][3]

Policy option: Suburban counties can require 20%+ tract poverty for mobile food vans and focus city help on CalFresh sign-ups.[1][2] Rural and valley counties can fund EBT delivery where high poverty meets 12-to-15-mile trips.[1][3]

How Food-Need Scores Help Counties Choose Food Benefits or Store Access

Measuring miles to the nearest grocer without checking pay misreads suburbs where hillside zoning limits shops. Across Santa Clara's 408 tracts and 514 SNAP stores, 93.4% of tracts (381) have centers within 1.0 mile of a store. The county mean trip is 0.573 miles. Of the 27 tracts past 1.0 mile, 26 are foothill tracts with under 20% poverty, such as Tract 5073.02 (2.970 miles, 3.7% poverty). This study checks for both 20%+ poverty and >1.0 mile from a tract center to a SNAP store. Only Tract 5116.08 (1.218 miles, 22.1% poverty, 3,252 residents) meets both rules.

Checking the five-part score shows that poverty accounts for 62.2% of score differences (+58.1 pp added, 60.6% partial). After poverty is included, store miles add just +0.003 pp (p = 0.827). Poverty and distance are both parts of that score. So this comparison describes current need rather than testing a new store. These findings support CalFresh sign-up help and post-rent food money in dense suburbs. Mobile markets and EBT delivery instead fit rural counties like Fresno (30 of 225 tracts meeting USDA rules, 13.3%, up to 15.53 mi) and Nevada (2.92 mi mean, 12.43 mi max).

Common Questions and Study Notes

Why does store distance explain 4.1% of score differences alone but add only 0.003 pp (p = 0.827) after poverty is included?
In Santa Clara County, 26 of the 27 tracts more than 1.0 mile from a SNAP store are in low-poverty hills. In the distance-only model, longer store trips link with lower food-need scores (4.1% of differences) because high-pay hillside zoning limits shops. Once tract poverty is included, store miles add just +0.003 pp of explained differences (0.009% partial, p = 0.827).
Why do the two measures of poverty's added information give 58.1 pp and 60.6%?
Both numbers show how much score difference poverty explains beyond store distance, using two totals. The full model explains 62.17% and distance alone explains 4.11%, so subtracting gives 58.06 pp (58.1 pp), called unique R-squared gain. Distance alone leaves 95.89% unexplained, and dividing 58.06 pp by 95.89% gives 60.55% (60.6%), called partial R-squared.
How do USDA low-income and low-access rules differ from this study's rules that identify 1 of 408 Santa Clara tracts?
The USDA Food Access Research Atlas marks low-income tracts by 20%+ poverty. Tracts also qualify if median family pay is at or below 80% of state or metro medians. At least 500 people or one-third must also live past 1 mile in cities (10 miles rural) from a large grocer. Under USDA rules, 15 of 408 Santa Clara tracts and 30 of 225 Fresno tracts (13.3%) qualify. This study's separate SNAP rules require both 20%+ poverty and a tract center past 1.0 mile from 514 SNAP stores. Only Tract 5116.08 (1.218 miles, 22.1% poverty, 3,252 residents) meets both rules.
