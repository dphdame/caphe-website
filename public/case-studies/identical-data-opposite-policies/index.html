Fixed Medi-Cal Visit Fees Do Not Meet City Clinic Costs While Poor Inland Areas Lack Care

Using clinic wage and rent scores to set fees and local poverty ranks to direct grants.

In practice today: State Medi-Cal fee rules pay one fixed visit rate from San Francisco to Imperial County.[1] In state talks, city clinics cite office wage and rent scores while inland groups cite local poverty.[1][2]

What California records show: Clinic cost scores reach 1.506 in San Francisco vs. 0.900 in Imperial, while hardship ranks place Imperial and Central Fresno at the 85th percentile vs. the 15th in San Francisco.[1][2] Separate national and 10-state studies find 17.6% Medicaid billing loss and visit gains from 58.7% to 66.4%.[3]

Policy option: State pay rules can adjust fees for clinic costs in high-rent cities and cut claim denials.[1][3] Separate loan-payoff and clinic grants can help hire doctors in 85th-percentile inland counties.[2][3]

• CMS Clinic Cost Score & Three-Part Shares: In the 2026 CMS doctor fee rule, three local cost parts sum to 34.44%. Staff wages are 19.15% (from federal wage data), office rent is 10.22% (from Census and housing data), and bought services are 5.07%. Dividing each part by 34.44% gives 55.6% wages, 29.7% office rent, and 14.7% bought services. The overall Practice Expense GPCI ranges from 1.506 in San Francisco to 0.900 in Imperial County.

• Billing Loss & Fee Increase Results: Dunn, Gottlieb, Shapiro, and Sonnenstuhl (cited by MACPAC, Jan 2025) report that Medicaid doctors lose 17.6% of claim value to denials and re-bills. That compares with 4.7% in Medicare and 2.4% in private plans. Polsky et al. (2015, NEJM) studied a 10-state Medicaid primary care fee increase to Medicare levels. Visit openings rose by 7.7 points, from 58.7% to 66.4% (+1.25 pp per 10% real fee increase).

Why Clinic Costs and Local Hardship Need Two Payment Rules

State Medi-Cal hearings often set coastal clinics against inland groups, yet both cite accurate data. Coastal clinics point to the CMS GPCI cost score. In CAPHE's three-part split, wages are 55.6%, rent is 29.7%, and services are 14.7%. That overall score is 1.506 in San Francisco (+50.6% above 1.00), and Santa Clara median rent is $2,814. Rural Nevada County is near 1.00 ($7,075 pay, $1,635 rent, 1.025 score). By contrast, ADI ranks place Imperial and Fresno Tract 2 ($3,103 pay, $1,007 rent) at the 85th hardship percentile. San Francisco ranks at the 15th percentile.

Using two payment rules matches state policy to each local problem. In a 10-state study, raising Medicaid fees raised visit openings from 58.7% to 66.4% (+7.7 points). Meanwhile, the Medicaid and CHIP Payment and Access Commission (MACPAC) reports a 17.6% Medicaid billing loss. Private plans lose 2.4%. Adjusting city fees for clinic costs and cutting claim denials helps pay urban rent and wages. Keeping the inland fee score at or above 1.00 and funding loan grants helps hire doctors in 85th-percentile counties.

Common Questions and Study Notes

How are staff wages, office rent, and bought services weighted in CAPHE's three-part cost comparison?
Under the 2026 CMS fee rule, three local cost shares make up 34.44% of the overall index: 19.15% staff wages, 10.22% office rent, and 5.07% bought services. Dividing each share by 34.44% scales those three parts to 100%, giving 55.6% wages, 29.7% rent, and 14.7% services. Separately, the overall clinic cost index is 1.506 in San Francisco and 0.900 in Imperial County.
Why do the CMS GPCI cost index and the ADI hardship index rank counties in opposite order?
The GPCI measures prices that clinics pay for staff wages and office rent (1.506 in San Francisco vs. 0.900 in Imperial). By contrast, the ADI ranks resident hardship using income, work, schooling, and housing (15th percentile in San Francisco vs. 85th in Imperial). City clinics pay high office rents and staff wages, while inland counties have high poverty and too few doctors.
How much claim value do Medicaid doctors lose to claim denials and billing paperwork?
National studies cited by MACPAC in 2025 report that Medicaid doctors lose 17.6% of claim value to denials and re-bills. That loss is more than triple Medicare's 4.7% billing loss. It is also over seven times the 2.4% loss in private plans.
