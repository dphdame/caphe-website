Routine County Health Spending Links With Lower Death Rates Outside Pandemic Grant Years

Testing 2020-2023 pandemic aid apart from pre-2020 county health spending across 57 counties.

Routine county health budgets fund local disease control and prevention year after year. During the 2020-2023 COVID-19 pandemic, state and federal agencies sent the largest emergency grants to counties with large increases in deaths. Higher death rates can bring in more emergency aid. That reverse causation means health needs affect spending rather than spending causing more deaths. Combining pandemic years with regular years makes the combined estimate close to zero because the two periods show opposite patterns. Testing the two periods apart lets county boards see how regular health spending links with death rates.

Across 943 yearly county records from 2003 to 2019, each extra $10 per resident in routine county health spending links to 2.38 fewer deaths per 100,000 per year.[1][2] Over 13-year changes, that link is 4.32 fewer deaths per 100,000 per year (unadjusted p = 0.0052; multiple-testing-adjusted p = 0.067, above 0.05).[1][2][3] A second statistical check estimates 5.31 fewer deaths per 100,000 per year.[3] Federal rules use $13.6M as the Value of a Statistical Life (VSL) to value reduced death risk. If causal, those three links imply $420,168 per expected death prevented ($32 per $1), $231,481 ($59 per $1), and $188,324.[2][3] Those dollar figures are illustrative valuations conditional on a causal interpretation, not cash received by counties.[2] By contrast, 2020-2023 emergency grants (220 county-years) rose 64.2% ($102.75 to $168.70) where COVID-19 deaths increased the most.[1][2] During those years, each $10 linked to +2.47 more deaths, putting all 2003-2023 years near zero at -0.25.[1][2]

• Pandemic Period Shift: From 2020 to 2023 (220 records), COVID-19 aid from state and federal agencies raised mean county health spending by 64.2%, from $102.75 to $168.70 per person. Over the same years, the all-cause death rate rose from 806.02 to 970.80 per 100,000. That produced a +2.47 per $10 link (p = 0.0010), which is 4.85 deaths per 100,000 above the -2.38 pre-COVID result.

• Urban, Rural & Cause-Specific Links: In 2003–2019 (943 records), higher spending links with lower death rates in urban counties above 500,000 people (-2.59 per $10, p < 0.0001). It also links with lower death rates in smaller counties at or below 500,000 people (-2.42, p = 0.0136) and in 2003–2016 (-2.62, p = 0.0128). Deaths from long-term illnesses show a -2.84 link (p = 0.0022). Heart and blood-vessel disease accounts for the largest share at -1.15 (p = 0.0066, or 48.4% of the yearly link).

• Where Results Come From: All estimates and test scores come from CAPHE's 57-county Local Health Authority study files and methodology notes.

Why Testing Crisis Aid Apart From Regular Budgets Makes Links Clear

County health spending rises after large death increases during emergencies, yet is linked to lower death rates in regular years. From 2020 to 2023, COVID-19 aid raised county health spending by 64.2%, from $102.75 to $168.70 per person. Funding increased most where pandemic deaths rose most, giving a +2.47 link (p = 0.001). Combining both periods in one 2003-2023 model mixes two opposite patterns. As a result, the combined estimate is close to zero at -0.25 (p = 0.710).

Analyzing the pre-COVID period (2003-2019, 943 records) on its own gives clear within-county comparisons. Each extra $10 per person links to 2.38 fewer deaths per 100,000 each year. Based on 13-year changes, that link is 4.32 fewer deaths per 100,000 each year (unadjusted p = 0.0052; adj. p = 0.067), alongside 5.31 in an extra check and 5.91 over 16 years. Using the $13.6M federal VSL value, those links imply $420,168 per expected death prevented ($32 per $1) yearly and $231,481 ($59 per $1) over 13 years. Those dollar estimates apply only if spending caused the lower death rates; they are not cash received by counties.

Common Questions and Study Notes

Why does 2020-2023 show +2.47 more deaths while 2003-2019 spending is linked to 2.38 fewer deaths?
From 2020 to 2023, state and federal COVID-19 aid raised county health spending by 64.2% ($102.75 to $168.70 per person). Funding rose most in counties with large increases in deaths, which explains why each $10 links to 2.47 more deaths per 100,000 in those years. Combining pandemic years with 2003-2019 gives an estimate near zero and obscures the earlier link with 2.38 fewer deaths.
Why does the 13-year model show 4.32 fewer deaths (p = 0.067 after adjustment) versus 2.38 in 1-year models?
The yearly model compares changes in spending and death rates inside each county from one year to the next (-2.38 per $10, including -1.15 from heart and blood-vessel disease, or 48.4%). The 13-year model compares changes over 13 years and estimates -4.32 (unadjusted p = 0.0052, adj. p = 0.067), alongside -5.91 over 16 years. Both models study observed records over different time spans, and neither proves cause and effect on its own.
Is regular county health spending linked to lower death rates in both urban and rural counties?
Yes, the 2003-2019 county records (943 county-years) show links between higher spending and fewer deaths in both groups. In urban counties above 500,000 residents, each extra $10 per person links to 2.59 fewer deaths per 100,000 (p < 0.0001). In smaller counties at or below 500,000 residents, each $10 links to 2.42 fewer deaths per 100,000 (p = 0.0136).
