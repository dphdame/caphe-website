Wide Error Ranges Before a Policy Can Miss Slow Differences in County Trends

Checking error ranges before a law and trend limits after it in two-group county tests.

In practice today: Before citing a two-group county study, reviewers check whether pre-law differences were near zero.[1][2] Yet short three-year pre-periods often have wide error ranges that miss slow trend differences.[2]

What the comparison shows: In an article-reported 1,408-county study (not publicly reproduced), pre-law checks pass (p = 0.9997) and give a -0.47 pp estimate.[1] Yet pre-law 95% error ranges span -1.27 to +1.16 pp, and the range includes zero once post-law trends differ by 0.35x of pre-law change.[1][2]

Policy option: Budget teams can ask for full pre-policy 95% error ranges and a 1.0x trend-bound check rather than a p-value alone.[2] Longer panels, such as California's 17-year 57-county records, give firmer pre-2020 history.[3]

• Low Precision of Wide-Error Pre-Tests (1,408 Counties). Pre-policy differences are -0.056 pp (SE = 0.620, 95% range [-1.27, +1.16]) at year -3, -0.030 pp (SE = 0.582, [-1.17, +1.11]) at year -2, and 0.000 pp (SE = 0.549, [-1.08, +1.08]) at year -1. Because those pre-policy ranges are more than 2.5 times wider than the -0.47 pp post-policy estimate, the joint pre-test (p = 0.9997) cannot rule out meaningful trend differences (Roth, 2022, AER: Insights).

• Rambachan-Roth 0.35x Trend Limit. Following Rambachan & Roth (2023, Review of Economic Studies), a 1.0x bound allows post-policy trend differences up to 100% of the largest pre-policy yearly slope change. On these inputs, the 95% bounded range reaches zero at 0.35x ([-0.96, 0.00] pp) and widens to [-1.08, +0.15] pp at 2.0x.

Why Passing a Pre-Trend Test Starts Rather Than Ends Study Review

Not finding a pre-law gap does not prove two county groups would have changed at the same rate. In the article-reported 1,408-county study (not publicly reproduced), pre-law differences are near zero (joint p = 0.9997). Assuming no later trend difference gives a -0.47 percentage-point drop (-0.91 to -0.03 pp). Yet pre-law 95% error ranges span -1.27 to +1.16 pp, over 2.5 times that drop. Because error ranges are wide, a slow trend difference big enough to explain the -0.47 pp drop still passes.

Adding Rambachan-Roth bounds replaces a pass-or-fail test with a clear numeric limit. Here, the 95% range includes zero once the post-law trend difference reaches 0.35x of the biggest pre-law change. Fitting 3-year county trend lines also moves the result to +0.003 pp. That 0.35x limit warns budget teams that a small trend difference removes the drop. Longer data, like California's 17-year, 57-county health records, give more pre-2020 history. Across those counties, $10 per resident links to 2.38 fewer yearly deaths and 4.32 fewer 13-year deaths per 100,000 (adj. p = 0.067).

Common Questions and Study Notes

Why does a high pre-trend p-value (such as p = 0.9997) not prove equal county trends?
A high p-value means only that the pre-policy data were too uncertain to rule out zero difference. Pre-policy 95% error ranges here span -1.27 to +1.16 percentage points. A county trend difference more than twice as large as the -0.47 pp estimate is still consistent with the pre-policy data.
What does a 0.35x post-policy trend limit mean for budget reviewers?
A 0.35x limit measures how small a post-policy trend difference can make the 95% range include zero. If the post-policy trend difference reaches 35% of the largest pre-policy change, zero enters the 95% range. Because 0.35x is well below 1.0x, even a modest trend difference can account for the -0.47 pp drop.
How does California's 57-county public health study compare with short 3-year county studies?
Following 57 counties across 17 pre-pandemic years (2003-2019) provides a much longer pre-2020 record. Each $10 per resident in routine health spending is linked to fewer deaths per 100,000 in urban (-2.59) and smaller (-2.42) counties. Statewide, spending is linked to 2.38 fewer yearly deaths and 4.32 fewer 13-year deaths (adj. p = 0.067).
