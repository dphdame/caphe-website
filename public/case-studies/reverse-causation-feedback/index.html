Combining Pandemic Aid With Routine County Health Budgets Mixes Relief Grants With Pre-2020 Links

Testing 2020-2023 pandemic grant years apart from pre-2020 county spending over 1 and 13 years.

In practice today: From 2020 to 2023, state and federal relief grants rose 64.2% where COVID-19 deaths rose most.[1][2] Mixing those relief years with pre-2020 budgets puts the 20-year link near zero (-0.25).[2]

What California records show: Across 943 pre-2020 county records (2003–2019), each $10 per person links with 2.38 fewer yearly deaths per 100,000.[1][2] Over 13 years, the link is 4.32 fewer deaths (adj. p = 0.067; 32.4:1 and 58.8:1 value if causal, not cash).[2][3]

Policy option: County boards can review 2020–2023 relief grants apart from routine health budgets.[1][2] Reports can list 2.38 and 4.32 (adj. p = 0.067) as observed links and mark death-risk values as if causal.[2][3]

How the Table Labels Match the Study Methods. The table labels refer to statistical models that compare observed county records across 57 counties without randomly assigning spending. Pandemic Emergency Aid Years (2020–2023) compares spending and death rates in the same years while accounting for lasting county differences and statewide yearly changes during COVID-19 relief grants. Yearly County Records Before COVID-19 (2003–2019) uses a two-way fixed-effects model for regular years before the pandemic. Changes Over 13 Years (2003–2016) compares 13-year spending changes with yearly death-rate changes under one financial reporting format (unadjusted p = 0.0052; p = 0.067 after adjustment for multiple tests). Second-Method Check applies Lewbel's 2012 method, following Brown (2016), using differences in how population traits vary across counties as an appendix check alongside Brown (2014) county government comparisons; this method relies on assumptions the data cannot fully test.

• Methods and County Groups. Across 57 counties in 2003-2019 (943 records), the county and year model gives 2.38 fewer deaths per 100,000 per $10 (SE = 0.78 per $10; -0.238 per $1, SE = 0.078 per $1, p = 0.0022; repeat-sample check p = 0.007). Links are also reported in large city counties (-2.59 per $10, p < 0.0001) and smaller counties (-2.42 per $10, p = 0.0136). On the 2003-2016 reporting form (779 records), the yearly link is -2.62 per $10 (p = 0.0128).

• Pandemic Aid Period and 13-Year Spans. Each model relates yearly county death rates to health spending while accounting for county traits, lasting county differences, and statewide yearly changes. In 2020-2023 (220 records), unadjusted spending rose 64.2% ($102.75 to $168.70 per person) during COVID-19 deaths and linked with +2.47 per $10 (SE = 0.75 per $10; +0.247 per $1, SE = 0.075 per $1, p = 0.0010). Comparing 13-year changes in 2003-2016 (57 counties) gives -4.32 per $10 (SE = 1.54 per $10, unadjusted p = 0.0052, adjusted p = 0.067). Over 16 years (2003-2019, 49 counties after dropping 5 reporting outliers), the link is -5.91 per $10 (SE = 2.77 per $10, p = 0.0330). The 13-year Lewbel two-step check gives -5.31 per $10 (SE = 1.67 per $10, p = 0.0015, first-step F = 111.20, Hansen J p = 0.2132). In log units, first-step F is only 0.85, so two-step models stay appendix checks.

• Why $1 and $10 Units Give the Same Dollar Estimates. Links per $1 per person (-0.238 yearly, -0.432 over 13 years with adjusted p = 0.067, and -0.531 in the two-step check) multiply both cost and deaths by 10 at the $10 scale (-2.38, -4.32, and -5.31). Because both sides multiply by 10, both units imply the same costs per death avoided if spending caused the change: $420,168 (32.4:1), $231,481 (58.8:1, adjusted p = 0.067), and $188,324 (72.2:1) at the $13.6M HHS value.

• Where the Data and Results Come From. Estimates come from the CAPHE Local Health Authority (LHA) calculator code, methods notes, and the 57-county California public health spending study.

Testing Pandemic Aid Years Apart From Pre-2020 County Spending

County health spending and death rates show two distinct links across time periods. From 2020 to 2023, average county spending rose 64.2% ($102.75 to $168.70 per resident) with a +2.47 death-rate link per $10 (p = 0.0010). Reverse causation is one possible reason because state and federal agencies sent relief grants to counties with large increases in deaths. When all years from 2003 to 2023 are combined, the link is -0.25 (p = 0.710). Testing the two periods apart keeps emergency grant years separate from routine budget links.

Checking pre-2020 budgets alone (943 county records) shows how spending links compare over 1 and 13 years. Yearly models report links with 2.38 fewer deaths per 100,000 per $10 across city (-2.59) and smaller (-2.42) counties. Based on 13-year changes, the link is 4.32 fewer deaths per 100,000 per year (unadjusted p = 0.0052; adjusted p = 0.067), and a two-step check gives 5.31. At the $13.6M federal value, these imply $420,168 per death avoided (32.4:1), $231,481 (58.8:1, adjusted p = 0.067), and $188,324 (72.2:1). These figures estimate health value only if spending caused fewer deaths, not county cash savings.

Common Questions and Study Notes

What is reverse causation in county health budgets, and when might it occur?
Reverse causation occurs when agencies send more health aid because local health worsened, rather than spending causing deaths. From 2020 to 2023, county spending rose 64.2% with a +2.47 death-rate link per $10 (p = 0.0010). Combining 2003 to 2023 gives -0.25. Relief grants to counties with large death increases are one possible reason, though models cannot prove grant timing caused it.
How do the $1 per resident spending links relate to the $10 per resident figures?
The study reports links per $1 spent per person: -0.238 per year, -0.432 over 13 years (adjusted p = 0.067), and -0.531 in two-step checks. Multiplying both cost and death-rate change by 10 gives -2.38, -4.32 (adjusted p = 0.067), and -5.31 per $10. This unit change does not alter death rates. Both units give the same dollar values if causal ($420,168 and $231,481).
How should county boards read $420,168 per death avoided, 32.4:1, and 58.8:1 (adj. p = 0.067)?
Those figures estimate resident health value only if spending caused lower death rates. They are not cash paid to county budgets. If causal, the -2.38 yearly link implies $420,168 per death avoided and 32.4:1 at $13.6M (15.0:1 to 49.3:1). Under that same rule, the -4.32 13-year link (adjusted p = 0.067) implies $231,481 and 58.8:1 (27.2:1 to 89.4:1, adjusted p = 0.067).
