Five-Year County Averages Separate Lasting Rural Death Risk From High One-Year Rates

Using five-year county averages and group size weights so rural health grants match lasting need.

In practice today: Grant programs sometimes rank counties on one year of death rates per 100,000 people.[2] In small rural counties, two or three extra deaths raise that rate sharply before returning toward average next year.[2]

What the comparison shows: In Alpine County (1,204 residents), one death changes the yearly rate by 83.06 points, and a count model estimates that 89.4% (-20.3 per 100K) of a top-10% 1-year rise returns toward average next year.[2] Across 17 pre-COVID years of county records, $10 per person links to -2.42 deaths per 100K in counties up to 500K (-4.32 over 13 years statewide, adj. p = 0.067).[1]

Policy option: State offices can rank rural grants on five-year averages weighted by county size (0.89 weight in tiny counties).[2][3] Pairing a base staffing grant with per-person aid keeps rural clinics funded across years.[1][3]

• How Population Size Affects Random Variation. Assume an average rate of 750 deaths per 100,000 people (0.0075). A Poisson count model estimates yearly random variation as 100,000 × √(0.0075 ÷ Population). That formula gives ±249.6 in Alpine (1,204), ±92.9 in Modoc (8,700), and ±68.3 in Trinity (16,060). Selecting counties in the highest 10% of 1-year random increases produces an expected -20.3 per 100,000 next-year decrease from return toward average (89.4% of an apparent -22.7 decrease).

• Smaller Rural and Urban County Results. Across 943 pre-COVID yearly county records (2003–2019), routine public health spending is linked to fewer deaths before age 75 in both smaller counties (≤500,000 residents: -2.42 per $10, p = 0.0136, 671 records) and large urban counties (>500,000 residents: -2.59 per $10, p < 0.0001). Statewide estimates are -2.38 each year, -4.32 over 13 years (p = 0.067 after multiple-test adjustment, above 0.05), and -5.31 in two-stage sensitivity checks (F = 111.20).

Why Five-Year Averages Improve Both Rural Grant Lists and Program Reviews

Giving short-term grants to counties with last year's highest death rate mistakes small population size for lasting need. Death rates report deaths for every 100,000 residents. One death in Alpine County (1,204 residents) changes its yearly rate by 83.06 points, vs. 0.01 in Los Angeles County. When a state office picks small counties after a high one-year rate, next year's rate is often closer to normal. That random increase is unlikely to repeat. The model assigns -20.3 of the -22.7 per 100,000 drop (89.4%) to regression to the mean.

Two simple rules keep random rural variation out of grant awards. First, 3-to-5-year means and size weights (89.0% pulled in tiny rural counties and 68.0% pulled in small rural counties) match grants to lasting need. Second, combining 17 pre-pandemic years across 57 counties (39,538,223 residents) estimates multi-year patterns. In counties with 500,000 or fewer residents, $10 per resident in routine health spending links to 2.42 fewer deaths per 100,000 each year. That figure is close to the statewide yearly (-2.38) and 13-year (-4.32, adj. p = 0.067) links.

Common Questions and Study Notes

Why do California's smallest rural counties appear among both the highest and lowest one-year death rates?
Death rates report deaths per 100,000 residents. In Alpine County (1,204 residents), one added death raises the rate by 83.06 per 100,000. In Los Angeles County (9.72 million residents), one death changes the rate by 0.01, so small counties are far more likely to have very high or low one-year rates.
How does regression to the mean create apparent program gains after a high one-year rate?
Counties picked after an unusually high Year-1 rate combine lasting health risk with random upward variation. In Year 2, that random error is unlikely to repeat. That expected return toward average produces a -20.3 per 100,000 decrease (89.4% of an apparent -22.7 drop) even if a grant had no effect.
Is routine health spending linked to lower death rates in smaller counties when several years are combined?
Yes, as an observational link across 17 pre-pandemic years (2003-2019). In counties with 500,000 or fewer residents, each $10 per resident in routine health spending is linked to 2.42 fewer deaths per 100,000 each year (p = 0.0136). That estimate is similar to the -2.38 statewide yearly link and the -4.32 13-year link (adj. p = 0.067).
