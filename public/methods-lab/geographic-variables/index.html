Household Poverty Accounts for Most Neighborhood Food-Access Variation Compared With Store Distance

We compare local poverty and SNAP rates with store distance across city, suburb, and rural census tracts.

When targeting food vans by distance to the nearest grocery store alone, a suburban team sends routes to car-owning foothill tracts while skipping low-pay renter areas near stores. Across 408 Santa Clara County tracts, household poverty accounts for 62.2% of the 5-part food index (a 58.1 pp unique R² gain, vs. 0.003 pp from distance, p = 0.827) because poverty and SNAP hold 55% of index weight and 93.4% of tracts sit within 1 mile.[1][2] Distance maps flag foothills (4.1% of spread) where 26 of 27 tracts past 1 mile have under 20% poverty.[1][2] Pairing poverty with trip miles separates urban outreach from Fresno (30 of 225 tracts, 13.3%).[1][2]

What Single-Year Tract Maps Can and Cannot Tell a County Team

A census-tract map summarizes where low-income households and grocery stores sit, but a single distance threshold can misclassify dense suburbs. In Santa Clara County (N = 408 tracts, 514 verified SNAP stores), 93.4% of tracts (381 of 408) sit within 1.0 mile of a SNAP store (mean 0.573 miles, median 0.409 miles). Of the 27 tracts beyond 1.0 mile, 26 are lower-poverty foothill areas (such as Tract 5073.02 at 2.97 miles and 3.7% poverty), while only 1 tract (Mountain View Tract 5116.08, 3,252 residents, 1.218 miles, 22.1% poverty) meets both USDA food-desert cutoffs.

Decomposing the county's 5-part vulnerability index (weighted 30% poverty, 25% SNAP, 20% store distance, 15% no-car share, and 10% density) shows that poverty accounts for 62.2% of index variance (+58.1 pp unique R² gain, 60.6% partial R²), whereas store distance adds +0.003 pp unique R² gain (p = 0.827) once poverty is included. Because this is an observational decomposition of a composite index in a store-dense county, it does not test the causal effect of building a store. Instead, it shows that suburban teams should pair poverty and SNAP rates with car access instead of screening by distance alone, while rural and valley counties with wider store gaps (such as Fresno County with 30 of 225 tracts at 13.3% USDA low-access up to 15.53 miles, and Nevada County averaging 2.92 miles up to 12.43 miles) require joint income-and-distance targeting.

Common Questions & Method Notes

Why did store distance show a negative single-factor R² of 4.1% if its unique R² gain after accounting for poverty is +0.003 pp (p = 0.827)?
That pattern reflects spatial sorting across Santa Clara County's 408 tracts. Exactly 26 of the 27 tracts located more than 1.0 mile from a SNAP store are lower-poverty foothill areas (under 20% poverty). In a single-factor regression on the composite index, distance acts as a proxy for foothill affluence. Once poverty rate is included, distance adds +0.003 pp of unique R² gain (0.009% partial R², p = 0.827).
What is the difference between semi-partial ΔR² (58.1 pp) and partial R² (60.6%), and what does the composite index measure?
The outcome is a 0-1 composite index weighted 30% poverty, 25% SNAP rate, 20% normalized store distance, 15% no-vehicle households, and 10% inverse density. Semi-partial ΔR² (58.06 pp, labeled Partial R² in the saved summary CSV) subtracts distance-only R² (4.11%) from full-model R² (62.17%). True partial R² divides that 58.06 pp gain by the 95.89% variance left unexplained by distance alone, giving 60.55% (60.6%).
Why was only 1 of 408 Santa Clara County tracts flagged as a USDA food desert compared with 30 of 225 tracts (13.3%) in Fresno County?
The USDA urban food-desert rule requires BOTH >=20% tract poverty AND >1.0 mile to a supermarket (>10 miles in rural tracts). Santa Clara County has 514 verified SNAP stores (mean distance 0.573 miles, 93.4% of tracts within 1.0 mile), so only Mountain View Tract 5116.08 (1.218 miles, 22.1% poverty, 3,252 residents) crosses both lines. Fresno County spans wider rural and agricultural tracts (mean 1.45 miles, max 15.53 miles), where 30 of 225 tracts (13.3%) cross both thresholds.
