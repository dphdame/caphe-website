Uniform Statewide Medi-Cal Fee Schedules Deliver Unequal Clinic Purchasing Power Across Counties

We compare local clinic costs with area need to match payment rates and staff grants.

When setting Medi-Cal clinic pay rates from one state list alone, a state agency sends funds to high-wage coastal cities or low-pay inland counties based on whether the rule tracks overhead or local poverty. Tying city clinic rates to the CMS Practice Expense GPCI backs coastal clinics where overhead runs 50.6% above the US mean (1.506 index in San Francisco vs. 0.900 in Imperial County).[1] One flat fee list leaves out cost and need gaps because San Francisco sits at the 15th percentile of local need while Imperial County sits at the 85th percentile.[2] Pairing cost rate boosts with loan grants and simpler billing (saving 17.6% claim value) fits both.[1][3]

Matching Cost Rate Indexing and Need-Targeted Staff Grants to Local Markets

In state budget hearings, coastal city health systems and inland rural groups show real federal data that rank counties in opposite ways. Coastal groups cite the CMS Practice Expense GPCI (where a CAPHE 3-part composite weights staff pay at 55.6% and office rent at 29.7%). San Francisco's Practice Expense GPCI sits at 1.506 (+50.6% above the US mean, with Santa Clara median rent at $2,814/month). Because the state pays one flat Medi-Cal fee list, coastal clinics face tight margins. Inland and border groups cite the Local Need Index (ADI) and Census ACS pay. Imperial County sits at the 85th US need percentile and Central Fresno homes earn $3,103/month ($1,007 rent), while San Francisco sits at the 15th percentile.

Health economics shows that these two rulers do not conflict. They track two separate market limits that need two separate tools. Polsky et al. (2015, NEJM) show that raising primary care fees lifts visit access by +7.7 pp (58.7% to 66.4%, or +1.25 pp per 10% fee bump). Dunn et al. / MACPAC (2025) show that 17.6% of Medicaid claim value is lost to billing hurdles (vs. 2.4% in private plans). Tying fee lists to clinic costs and cutting the 17.6% billing loss backs high-cost city clinics. Sending CalHealthCares loan pay and residency grants by local need builds doctor supply in inland counties.

Mode B Observed Benchmarks: All CMS CY 2026 Practice Expense GPCI scores (1.506 San Francisco vs. 0.900 Imperial) and CAPHE 3-part normalized cost shares (55.6% pay, 29.7% rent, 14.7% estimated services from the 19.15%, 10.22%, and 5.07% MEI shares) are Mode B data. They pair with Area Deprivation Index percentiles (15th vs. 85th) and Census ACS 5-Year (2019-2023) rents ($2,814, $2,318, $1,635, $1,007) and monthly pay ($13,306, $5,507, $7,075, $3,103) across different census universes. Common Questions & Method Notes

How does the CAPHE 3-part composite of the CMS Practice Expense GPCI weight local pay and rent?
Under the CMS CY 2026 Physician Fee Schedule Final Rule, three Practice Expense GPCI sub-components (19.15% local health staff wages from BLS OEWS, 10.22% office rent from Census ACS 2-bedroom gross rent, and 5.07% estimated purchased services) normalize across their 34.44% sum to 55.6% staff pay, 29.7% rent, and 14.7% estimated services. The overall Practice Expense GPCI is 1.506 in San Francisco vs. 0.900 in Imperial County.
Why do the CMS Practice Expense GPCI and the Area Deprivation Index rank San Francisco and Imperial in opposite ways?
The CMS Practice Expense GPCI tracks what a clinic pays to hire staff and rent space (1.506 in SF vs. 0.900 in Imperial). The Neighborhood Need Index tracks resident poverty and hardship (15th percentile in SF vs. 85th percentile in Imperial). High-cost cities squeeze clinic margins, while high-need rural counties face low family pay and doctor shortages.
How much does billing friction cut real Medicaid pay?
Work by Dunn, Gottlieb, Shapiro, and Sonnenstuhl cited by MACPAC (January 2025) shows that Medicaid providers lose 17.6% of claim value to denials and billing hurdles. That is more than triple Medicare (4.7%) and seven times private plans (2.4%).
