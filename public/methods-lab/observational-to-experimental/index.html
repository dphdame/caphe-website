Routine County Public Health Budgets Lower Premature Deaths After Separating Outbreak Spending

We compare raw yearly spending averages with multi-year routine budget models across California counties.

When evaluating county public health budgets by same-year death rates, an analyst sees flat results because emergency outbreak grants arrive in high-death years. Tracking routine budget shifts across 1,197 county-years (2003 to 2023) shows that each $10 per resident in county health spending prevents 9.16 deaths per 100,000 ($125 in health value per $1 spent).[1][2] A raw yearly average across counties shows +0.18 deaths per 100,000 because boards send surge funds when death rates spike.[1][2] Matching this 9.16 result within 99.4% of an earlier 40-county board study (9.10 fewer deaths per 100,000) gives boards two separate checks across two decades.[3]

• Cross-Decade Match (Mode B): We compare the 2001-2008 county board result (-9.10) on 40 counties with the 2003-2023 routine-budget result (-9.16) on 57 counties. The match is 1 - |-9.16 - (-9.10)| / 9.16 = 99.34% (~99.4% match) across two methods and time windows.

• Regression & Lag Checks (Mode B): In 2SLS, raw averages give SE = 0.40 (t = 0.46, p = 0.644, 95% CI -0.60 to +0.97). The variance-based IV model gives SE = 1.11. Two checks back up splitting base and surge funds. First, a lag model (N = 684) finds a positive 2-year lag (+1.58, SE = 0.49, p = 0.001) when counties raise pay after death shocks. Second, a Breusch-Pagan test (LM = 91.68, F = 12.32, p < 0.001) rejects equal error spread. Results hold in 2003-2019 pre-COVID data (4.56 fewer deaths***, N = 969, 62:1 BCR), rural counties (8.11 fewer deaths**), and 46 of 47 checks (leave-one-out SD = 0.05, range -9.21 to -9.02).

Why Two Separate Checks Across Two Decades Build Budget Trust

County boards and state lawmakers set health budgets in real time. They send surge funds to counties facing disease outbreaks or death spikes. Because surge cash flows to high death rates, a raw yearly average (+0.18 deaths per 100,000, p = 0.64) mixes emergency aid with base care. That makes strong county programs look flat.

Splitting base budgets from outbreak surges shows the true value of county health teams. Two studies across two decades sit within 0.06 deaths per 100,000 of each other. Brown's 2001-2008 study found 9.10 fewer deaths per 100,000. CAPHE's working-paper 2003-2023 study across 57 counties found 9.16 fewer deaths per 100,000 (F = 314.1). It also found a 2.9x larger result for 2010-2023 than for 2003-2009 (8.93 vs. 3.07 fewer deaths). Budget leaders can back base prevention with clear proof.

Mode B Panel & Published Studies: All slopes (+0.18, -9.10, -9.16, -3.07, -8.93), standard errors (0.40, 1.11), and checks (F = 314.1, Hansen J = 4.49, p = 0.343) are Mode B estimates. They come from the 57-county working-paper panel (N = 1,197, excluding Berkeley and Pasadena city health units) and published studies.

Common Questions & Method Notes

Why does the raw yearly average (+0.18) have the opposite sign of the routine budget result (9.16 fewer deaths)?
County boards and state teams send emergency health funds when death rates spike, such as during flu waves or COVID-19. That outbreak funding shift (+9.34 deaths/100K) offsets the 9.16 protective effect in raw comparisons.
Why does the match between the 2001-2008 study and the 2003-2023 panel matter?
The 2001-2008 study used county board rules across 40 counties (9.10 fewer deaths). The 2003-2023 panel used routine budget shifts across 57 counties (9.16 fewer deaths). A 99.4% match across two methods, decades, and samples rules out model quirks.
Why is the result 2.9 times larger for 2010-2023 than for 2003-2009?
Splitting the panel at 2010 gives 3.07 fewer deaths/100K in 2003-2009 and 8.93 fewer in 2010-2023 per $10 spent. Wider insurance under the 2014 Medi-Cal expansion may help people act on county health screenings. Yet the later window also spans the post-2009 recovery and COVID-19, so the era split alone cannot isolate insurance.
