Minimum Group-Size Filters and Multiple-Testing Adjustments Focus Outreach on Projected Coverage Losses

We filter small-group swings across modeled county subgroup benchmarks so outreach funds target the largest plan drops.

When scanning 62 demographic and language cells on a monthly dashboard, a county team risks ranking random swings in tiny groups over large headcount drops. Pairing a modeled 62-group screen across 62 Santa Clara County Medi-Cal groups with total headcount focuses outreach on Spanish-speaking members (-24,880 net drop, or 41.3% of the county drop) and Hispanic adults ages 19 to 44 (-17,380).[1][2] Sorting those 62 groups by raw percent drop puts tiny groups first, like Samoan (-34.04%, 47 to 31 members) and French (-25.00%, 56 to 42), where a few moving homes shift the rate.[1] Setting a size floor (100+ members) with modeled drops past the county rate guides staff.[1][2]

Why Health Teams Pair Size Floors and Multi-Test Checks With Net Headcount Scale

When a county health team tracks Medi-Cal renewal drops across 62 age, sex, and language groups (472,014 to 411,722 members in Santa Clara County; -60,292 or -12.77% net change), two common dashboard habits pull staff off course. First, sorting groups by raw percent drop puts tiny groups at the top. Samoan (-34.04%, 47 to 31 members; -16 people), French (-25.00%, 56 to 42; -14 people), and Thai (-20.75%, 53 to 42; -11 people) rank above Spanish (-18.00%, Rank #11 by percent). Yet Spanish-speaking homes account for 24,880 net lost members, or 41.3% of the entire county drop. Second, running 62 unadjusted tests at α = 0.05 produces 3.10 expected false leads by chance alone, and treating net monthly snapshot counts as independent member exits misstates uncertainty.

Pairing a size floor (n ≥ 100), valid multiple-testing checks (such as replicate-weight pairwise tests, Holm step-down, or Benjamini-Hochberg FDR where sampling variance is observed), and net headcount drop past the -12.77% county rate links statistical care with real scale. Once small cells go to partner outreach and large groups are ranked by net drop past the county rate, two major priorities stand out. First is Spanish-speaking homes (-24,880 net drop; -7,230 past the county rate). Second is young adults ages 19 to 44 (Hispanic 19-44 at -20.11%, -17,380 net drop and -6,344 past the county rate; Male 19-44 at -20.19%, -15,496 net drop and -5,697 past the county rate).

Observed 62-Cell DHCS Grid, Mode A Multiple Testing Rules & Mode B Benchmarks: All 62 group counts in June 2025 (472,014 total) and June 2026 (411,722 total), net headcount changes (-60,292 total; -24,880 Spanish; -17,380 Hispanic 19-44; -15,496 Male 19-44; -16 Samoan; -14 French; -11 Thai), and percent shifts come from scc_hr1_medicaid/scc_dhcs_medical_enrollment.csv. Expected false leads (62 × 0.05 = 3.10), Bonferroni cutoff (0.05 / 62 = 0.00081), and net drops past the -12.77% county rate are Mode A calculations. Replicate-weight PUMA pairwise tests (p = 0.469 to 0.730 in scc_puma_pairwise_tests.json), 16-horizon Holm/FDR adjustments (raw p = 0.0049 to Holm p = 0.0788 in Value_of_Public_Health/paper/ajph_submission.tex), and the 1,000-shuffle permutation test (null max AUC = 0.593, p = 0.001 in Mcaid/05_fraud_detection/paper/output/results.json) are Mode B benchmarks.

Common Questions & Method Notes

Why do small language cells like Samoan (-34.04%) and French (-25.00%) sit at the top of raw percent tables?
In a group with just 47 members (Samoan) or 56 members (French), a net shift of 14 to 16 people (often three or four families moving or changing jobs) swings the cell rate by 25% to 34%. Without a size floor (such as n >= 100) and headcount weights, raw percent lists mostly show small-sample swings.
Why can't we run simple binomial exit p-values on monthly DHCS enrollment snapshots, and how do Holm or FDR gates work on valid tests?
Monthly DHCS snapshots show net stock changes (exits minus entries), overlap across age and language tables, and cluster by family, so individual exit counts are not observed. When valid sampling errors exist, such as the 80-replicate-weight PUMA tests (pairwise p = 0.469 to 0.730) or the 16-horizon public health spending check (where raw p = 0.0049 shifts to Holm p = 0.0788 and FDR q = 0.0788), Bonferroni, Holm, and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR prevent false leads.
How is the Net Drop Past the County Rate calculated and why should dashboards show it alongside size floors?
It compares a group's observed net change against what would happen at the county net rate (-12.77%). For Spanish-speaking members (138,215 base), a -12.77% shift equals -17,650 members. The observed net drop of -24,880 leaves a -7,230 gap past the county rate (-7,225 unrounded). That shows where bilingual outreach staff can help the most families keep care.
