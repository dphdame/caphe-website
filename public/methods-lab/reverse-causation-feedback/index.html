Same-Year County Spending Averages Combine Routine Prevention Budgets With Outbreak Emergency Funds

Splitting emergency outbreak fund surges from steady base prevention budgets across county years.

When a county board audits public health spending and death rates in the same year, the review mixes steady base prevention with emergency funds added after past spikes. Across 1,197 California county-years (2003 to 2023), routine-budget models isolate a 9.16 fewer deaths per 100,000 slope, equal to $109,170 per life saved on the LHA calculator's $10-per-resident scale (or $10,919 on the working paper's $1 table scale).[1][2] Same-year averages show a flat +0.18 slope because counties raise budgets after death spikes, with a +1.58 two-year lag slope.[2] Checking the -4.56 calm-year floor and the -9.16 full-panel slope protects multi-year grants from early cuts.[3]

• Estimator Details & Robustness (Mode B): The working-paper IV estimate uses Lewbel (2012) variance-based instruments built from first-stage residuals. Diagnostics show first-stage F = 314.1, Breusch-Pagan LM = 91.68 (F = 12.32, p < 0.001), and Hansen J = 4.49 (p = 0.343). Brown (2014) yields a close -9.10 per $10/capita (-0.91 per $1/capita). Across 47 model checks, 46 pass at p < 0.05 (97.9% pass rate). The leave-one-county-out SD is 0.05 (range -9.21 to -9.02). The 2003-2019 pre-pandemic subsample (N = 969) yields -4.56, and the small-county subsample yields -8.11.

• Simultaneous Equations & Distributed Lag Evidence (Mode B): In the system Mortality_it = β·Spending_it + γ·X_it + α_i + τ_t + ε_it and Spending_it = θ·Mortality_it + Π·Z_it + u_it, needs-driven funding (θ > 0) and unobserved confounding create a 9.34-point arithmetic gap between the same-year OLS slope (+0.184, SE = 0.399, p = 0.644) and the IV slope (-9.158, SE = 1.108, p < 0.001). That 9.34-point gap is the difference between the two estimators, not a separately identified causal effect of outbreak spending. Direct distributed-lag testing (N = 684, joint F = 6.73, p = 0.001) finds a current-year slope of -0.919 (SE = 0.284, p = 0.001) and a positive 2-year lag slope of +1.581 (SE = 0.491, p = 0.001), showing that counties raise public health budgets about two years after mortality shocks. In the 2003-2016 consistent SCO series, 5-year first differences (-1.561 OLS, p < 0.001; -2.996 IV, p = 0.002, N = 500) and 13-year differences (-2.947 OLS vs. -3.476 IV, p < 0.001, N = 57) also turn negative once short-run budget feedback is differenced out.

• Unit Convention Reconciliation ($10 Calculator Scale vs. $1 Regression Table Scale): As documented in GOAL_2026-06-18_value_deck_v2.md (L31-39), the working-paper files contain two unit conventions for the -9.158 coefficient. In Value_of_Public_Health/paper/sections/lewbel_iv_results_section.tex (L18-20 and Table 5, L298-303), the regressor is labeled per $1/capita (-9.158 per $1/capita, or -91.58 per $10/capita; +0.184 OLS per $1/capita, or +1.84 per $10/capita), which yields a cost per life saved of $1 × 100,000 / 9.158 = $10,919 ($21,930 at -4.56; $12,330 at -8.11) and a 1,246:1 BCR at $13.6M VSL. However, Section 4.4 of the same paper (L336-338: $10/capita [$390M] across 39M residents saving 3,572 lives = $109,000/life and 125:1 BCR), public/tools/lha-calculator/method.html (L231-357), and src/frontend/js/lha-calculator.js (L40-47: coefficientPer10 = -9.16, coefficient = -0.916 per $1) evaluate -9.16 on the conservative $10/capita scale ($10 × 100,000 / |β| = $109,170/life, 124.6:1 BCR; 177.4 lives across 1,936,259 Santa Clara residents for a $19.36M [$10/capita] outlay, or for a $1.94M [$1/capita] outlay under the $1 table scale). Table 1 reports both scales side by side and uses the conservative $10 calculator scale as the primary policy benchmark.

Splitting Outbreak Aid From Steady Prevention Gains

Every public health budget works inside a feedback loop. When deaths or disease outbreaks rise, boards add funds over the next budget cycle. In the 57-county panel, distributed-lag models show a positive +1.58 slope at a two-year lag (p = 0.001) as counties respond to past death spikes. Because spending reacts to sickness, simple same-year averages (+0.18, p = 0.644) mix reactive funding and confounding with steady prevention.

Health economics separates the direction of cause across both calm and pandemic windows. Routine-budget IV models isolate funding shifts that happen apart from local outbreaks, finding a 9.16 fewer deaths per 100,000 slope across the full 2003-2023 panel (F = 314.1; 125:1 return on the conservative $10 calculator scale, or 1,246:1 on the paper's $1 table scale). Excluding the 2020-2023 pandemic years still yields -4.56 (62:1 calculator return), and small counties show -8.11 (110:1 calculator return). Showing boards both the calm-year floor and the full-panel return helps keep effective programs funded across multi-year budget cycles.

Common Questions & Method Notes

What is "reverse causation" in public health budgets?
It happens when the health outcome (death or disease rates) causes changes in the budget input (public health spending). In the California county panel, distributed-lag tests show a positive two-year lag slope (+1.58, p = 0.001) because counties raise public health budgets after death spikes. That reactive pattern and unobserved confounding create a 9.34-point gap between the same-year OLS slope (+0.18) and the IV estimate (-9.16).
Why does Table 1 show both a $10 calculator scale and a $1 regression table scale?
In the working paper (lewbel_iv_results_section.tex), Tables 1-5 label the -9.158 slope per $1/capita ($10,919 per life saved, 1,246:1 BCR at $13.6M VSL), while Section 4.4 of the paper and the CAPHE LHA Calculator (lha-calculator.js) evaluate -9.16 per $10/capita (-0.916 per $1/capita) to match Brown (2014)'s -9.10 per $10/capita scale ($109,170 per life saved, 125:1 BCR). Reporting both scales shows that county public health spending is highly cost-effective under either unit convention.
Does county public health spending still pay off if a board uses only the pre-pandemic calm-year floor?
Yes. Using the observed 2003-2019 pre-pandemic estimate of 4.56 fewer deaths per 100,000 on the conservative $10 calculator scale yields $219,298 per life saved and a 62.0:1 benefit-cost ratio at the $13.6M HHS central VSL (or $21,930 per life and 620:1 on the $1 table scale).
