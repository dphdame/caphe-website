Modeled County Public Health Prevention Clears Cost-Neutral Thresholds Across All Federal Valuation Standards

Testing modeled returns across low, middle, and high federal life value tiers. Checking both point estimates and lower bounds.

County boards and budget officers ask whether a public health program still pays off under cautious assumptions. Federal agencies publish low, middle, and high dollar values for each saved life alongside different hospital savings rules. Checking program returns across all of those cases shows which funding choices stay above breakeven.

Across all three federal tiers ($6.3M, $13.6M, and $20.7M per life), California county public health spending yields a modeled 15.0:1 to 49.3:1 annual return at 2.38 fewer deaths per 100,000 and a 27.2:1 to 89.4:1 cumulative return at 4.32 fewer deaths per 100,000.[1][2] Using one middle number (32.4:1 annual or 58.8:1 cumulative at $13.6M) leaves a plan open to debate over one input.[2] Showing that the lowest joint bound still yields 5.4:1 annually (8.2:1 cumulatively) confirms that the spending pays off under every federal rule.[3]

Why the Goal of Sensitivity Analysis Is Decision Robustness, Not Parameter Perfection

County fiscal reviews test whether a public health investment remains cost-effective across conservative and upper-bound federal valuation tiers: should the analysis use the U.S. EPA $11.2M Value of a Statistical Life, the HHS $13.6M central benchmark, or the $6.3M HHS low tier? When an analyst presents only a single point estimate (32.4:1 annual or 58.8:1 cumulative), any disagreement over one input casts doubt on the entire recommendation.

Sensitivity analysis reframes the conversation around which assumptions change the decision. Across the HHS 2025 VSL range, county public health spending returns 15.0:1 to 49.3:1 annually and 27.2:1 to 89.4:1 over 13-year cumulative horizons, and the joint floor (lower confidence bound at $6.3M) still returns 5.4:1 annually and 8.2:1 cumulatively, so the funding verdict does not depend on the VSL tier or exposure horizon. CHW cost capture behaves differently: direct FFS billing recovers 6.5% to 9.8% of a $103,719 position and payer offsets recover 103%, so for CHW programs the financing perspective, not the valuation tier, determines whether the budget closes.

Common Questions & Method Notes

What is the difference between one-way and multi-way sensitivity analysis?
One-way sensitivity analysis varies a single parameter at a time while holding all other inputs at their central values (for example, varying VSL from $6.3M to $20.7M at the -2.38 annual effect size, yielding 15.0:1 to 49.3:1, or 27.2:1 to 89.4:1 at the -4.32 cumulative effect size). Multi-way sensitivity analysis varies two or more uncertain parameters together (such as combining the $6.3M Low VSL with the -0.85 annual or -1.30 cumulative lower 95% confidence bound to test a 5.4:1 to 8.2:1 conservative joint floor).
What is a "cost-neutral threshold analysis" in cost-benefit modeling?
Cost-neutral analysis asks how small the program effect would have to be for benefits to just equal costs (BCR = 1.0:1). At the $13.6M HHS Central VSL, a $10/capita investment reaches cost neutrality if it averts 0.0735 deaths per 100,000 residents, meaning the observed -2.38 annual effect is 32 times larger (and the -4.32 cumulative effect is 59 times larger) than the cost-neutral minimum.
Why are CHW cost-capture scenarios reported in separate rows from VSL-based returns?
The two measure different units. VSL-based returns value mortality reductions from county public health spending, while CHW cost capture compares cash or resource offsets with the $103,719 annual cost of one California CHW position. Direct FFS billing recovers 6.5% to 9.8% of that cost and Medi-Cal payer offsets recover about 103%, so the CHW financing question depends on who captures the savings instead of on the VSL tier.
