Unaffected Comparison Groups Separate Program-Specific Provider Growth From System-Wide Enrollment Surges

Comparing treated and comparison care lines to separate modeled policy shifts from broad enrollment waves.

When evaluating a county Medi-Cal service rollout by tracking only enrolled clinics, a single-group review credits statewide enrollment waves to the local pilot. Comparing telehealth-eligible Medicaid services against other Medicaid care across 84 months (2018 to 2024) isolates a modeled net +5.62 percentage point provider gain through 2023 and +4.60 percentage points through 2024.[1] A single-group pre-post check credits all +24.33% growth to telehealth without subtracting the +18.71% enrollment wave that lifted other care at the same time.[2] Pairing the two-group check with the +1.21-point pre-2020 base gap bounds the budget forecast.[3]

Two-Ruler Benchmark Summary. Mode A Two-Group Check on HHS Medicaid Provider Spending (84 Months, Jan 2018 to Dec 2024). Ruler 1: Single-Group Pre-Post Change (Includes Shared Medicaid Waves): +22,239 Providers (+24.33%). Video care in Medicaid averaged 91,399 monthly billing providers in 2019 and 113,638 in 2023 (+22,239, +24.33%). That raw jump mixes broad enrollment growth, stay-enrolled rules, and any video pay shift into one count. Ruler 2: Mode A Two-Group Gap vs. Other Medicaid Care: +5.62 pp (2023); +4.60 pp (2024). In the Mode A two-group check, subtracting the other-care gain (+18.71%) leaves +5.62 pp (24.33 − 18.71) through 2023 and +4.60 pp (11.57 − 6.97) through 2024. The 2018 to 2019 growth gap was already +1.21 pp (8.26 − 7.05). Part of the post-2019 gap may continue that earlier trend.

How Two-Group Comparisons Guide Budget Choices

During broad shifts like the 2020 to 2024 enrollment wave and Medicaid redetermination, almost every Medicaid count moves at once. If an agency credits telehealth policy with the raw +24.33% rise in billing providers, it cannot tell if that gain came from new billing rules or a system-wide enrollment surge.

A two-group comparison (Side-by-Side Group Comparison, or DiD) uses a second care line on the same timeline, here other Medicaid care (+18.71%), to subtract the shared shift. That step changes the budget takeaway. The +24.33% raw gain narrows to +5.62 pp through 2023 and +4.60 pp through 2024. Extending the +1.21-point pre-2020 gap trims it to +0.78 pp and -1.45 pp. The result rests on how closely the two groups tracked before the rule change.

Mode Classification (Mode A Two-Group Check on Observed Series): Billing provider counts and paid dollars come from the HHS Medicaid Provider Spending file (telehealth_month_summary.parquet, Jan 2018 to Dec 2024), split by whether a code allows telehealth billing. Net two-group gaps and pre-trend bounds are Mode A calculations on these observed series. Unit of analysis: care-group-month (2 groups × 84 months = 168 rows).

Primary Data Sources: U.S. HHS Medicaid Provider Spending File, Medicaid.gov Open Data, CMS T-MSIS Analytic Files Documentation, and California DHCS Telehealth Policy Guidance.

Common Questions & Method Notes

How does a two-group comparison (DiD) separate policy shifts from a shared wave?
It subtracts the before-and-after change in a comparison group hit by the same wave (other Medicaid care, +18.71% from 2019 to 2023) from the change in the treated group (video care, +24.33%). That leaves a +5.62 percentage-point net gap.
Why does the 2019-to-2024 net gap (+4.60 pp) differ from the 2019-to-2023 gap (+5.62 pp)?
From 2023 to 2024, video-care providers fell 10.26% and other-care providers fell 9.89%. Both drops reflect Medicaid redetermination and unpaid Nov and Dec 2024 claims.
What must hold for other Medicaid care to work as a valid comparison group?
Both care groups must follow parallel paths when no new video rule is enacted. In 2018 to 2019, the treated group grew 1.21 points faster. Extending that pre-2020 gap trims the 2019-to-2023 net gap to +0.78 pp.
