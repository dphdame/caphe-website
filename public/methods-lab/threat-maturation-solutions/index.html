Multi-Year Trend Splits Separate Ongoing Workforce Growth From Projected State-Law Benefit Jumps

Separating steady yearly payroll growth from modeled level and slope shifts after state paid-leave laws.

When evaluating a statewide Paid Family Leave pay increase without a multi-year base trend, a one-year review mixes routine yearly claim growth into the first-year policy jump. In California Paid Family Leave, multi-year trend modeling projects +39,600 added yearly claims and +$623.7M per year in gross outlays from a 12-week plan while reserves rebuild to 30.4% ($6.37B) by 2030.[1] Raw year-to-year spending checks mix that policy jump with +$1.635B in routine wage and workforce growth (325,055 to 330,000 base claims and $1,090 to $1,165 weekly pay).[2] Tracking both the Year-1 level shift and the 5-year reserve slope keeps payroll tax rates steady.[3]

Why Multi-Year Trend Splits Track Both the Year-1 Dip and the 5-Year Slope

When a state passes a law like Senate Bill 951, every covered worker in California enters the new rule on the same day. Because there is no unexposed part of the state, analysts use a multi-year trend model (Multi-Year Trend Split, or ITS) across historical and forecast series. A simple two-year check cannot split policy from trend when wages, worker counts, and tax bases are already climbing.

Splitting the base slope (+1.52% claim growth, +6.88% weekly wage growth, and the post-2024 uncapped tax slope that lifts base reserves from 18.1% to 50.7%) from the modeled 12-week jump (+39,600 claims, +$623.7M/yr outlay) clarifies the budget choice. A 1-year view in 2026 shows the reserve ratio dipping to 10.8% ($1.96B balance). Yet the post-shift slope through 2030 shows the uncapped wage base rebuilding the fund to $6.37B (30.4%, above EDD's 25% floor) without going negative.

Mode Classification (Mode A Projections on Observed EDD Baselines): Year 2025 figures (325,055 PFL claims, $1,090.00 weekly pay, $15.892B SDI payouts, $3.011B fund balance, 18.9% reserve ratio) are observed EDD actuals. Years 2026 to 2030 base and expansion series, including the level-and-slope split, are Mode A projections from /public/tools/pfl-simulator/data/pfl_model_data.json anchored to the EDD May 2026 DI Fund Forecast.

Common Questions & Method Notes

How does a multi-year trend model (ITS) differ from a simple before-and-after check?
A simple before-and-after check subtracts one pre-policy year from one post-policy year and mixes ongoing growth with the policy shift. A multi-year trend model tracks the pre-policy base slope (+1.52% claim growth and +6.88% wage growth) and measures both the Year-1 jump and the post-policy slope.
Why does the California SDI reserve ratio rise from 18.1% in 2025 to 50.7% by 2030 in the base forecast?
SB 951 removed the wage tax cap on SDI employee taxes starting January 1, 2024 (taxing all wages across a $1,371.5B base). Weekly benefit payouts stay capped ($1,765/wk in 2026), so base tax revenue grows faster than base payouts.
What happens to the SDI fund reserve under the Expected 12-week PFL plan?
In 2026, the +$623.7M outlay lowers the reserve ratio to 10.8% ($1.96B vs. $3.19B base). The post-SB 951 tax slope then rebuilds the fund balance each year, reaching $6.37B (30.4% reserve ratio, above EDD's 25% floor) by 2030.
