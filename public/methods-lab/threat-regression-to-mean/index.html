Multi-Year Pooled Baselines Separate Persistent Rural Mortality From Projected Small-County Bounce-Back

Separating lasting county health gains from modeled regression to the mean after a one-year spike.

When targeting multi-year rural health grants to whichever small counties had the highest one-year death rate, funding chases brief one-year spikes instead of lasting need. Across 21 years of California county records (2003 to 2023), steady public health funding averts 8.11 deaths per 100,000 residents per $10 per person in small rural counties and 9.16 statewide.[1] Ranking small counties on one year of data overstates next-year gains through modeled regression to the mean. Just 1 death in Alpine County (1,204 residents) swings the rate by 83.1 per 100,000 and accounts for 71% of a -28.4 per 100,000 bounce-back.[2] Funding rural grants on 3-to-5-year pooled averages sends dollars to lasting local need.[3]

• Small-County Noise Math (Mode A on DOF Populations): At a base death rate of μ = 750 per 100,000 (0.0075), the 1-year Poisson SD per 100,000 is σ = 100,000 × √(0.0075 / N_pop). That equals ±249.6 per 100,000 in Alpine (N = 1,204), ±92.9 in Modoc (N = 8,700), and ±68.3 in Trinity (N = 16,060).

• Drop-One-County Check (Observed Source Estimates): Across all 57 counties over 2003 to 2023 (N = 1,197), dropping one county at a time yields a slope SD of just 0.052. Rural outliers do not skew the -9.16 panel result (CAPHE LHA Methodology).

Why Base Funding Beats Chasing One-Year Spikes

Regression to the mean is a common hurdle in county grant design. Suppose an agency ranks California's 58 counties on one year of death data and sends short-term grants to the top tenth. Small rural counties under 25,000 residents top the list when a few random deaths swing their rate per 100,000 by 50 to 250 points. Next year, the rate drops back toward its long-run mean (-28.4 per 100,000) even without a program effect.

Health economics fixes this funding problem in two steps. First, for grant targeting, Empirical Bayes shrinkage and 3-to-5-year pooled means pull noisy small-county rates toward regional norms. Second, for impact review, 21-year panel models across all 57 counties (N = 1,197 county-years) show that steady public health funding in small counties averts 8.11 deaths per 100,000 per $10/person, close to the statewide 9.16 result.

Mode Classification (Mode A on County Headcounts + Observed Panel Estimates): County resident counts (Alpine 1,204 to Los Angeles 9,721,138), the small-county IV estimate (-8.11), the full-panel IV estimate (-9.16, SE = 1.11, N = 1,197), and the drop-one-county SD (0.052) are observed values in the CAPHE LHA corpus. One-year Poisson noise SDs, the bounce-back share, and shrinkage weights (B_i) are Mode A calculations on those county headcounts.

Common Questions & Method Notes

Why do small rural counties sit at the top and bottom of one-year death rankings?
Death rates are scaled per 100,000 residents. One death in Alpine County (1,204 residents) moves the rate by 83.06 per 100,000. One death in Los Angeles County (9.72 million residents) moves the rate by 0.01 per 100,000.
How does regression to the mean skew pre-post reviews of one-time grants?
When counties get grants because they had a top-decile death spike in Year 1, that peak includes random upward noise. In Year 2, the random error averages zero and creates a modeled -20.3 per 100,000 bounce-back even with zero program effect.
What does the 57-county panel show for public health funding in small counties?
Across 21 years (2003 to 2023), the published IV panel model links every $10 per person in local public health funding to 8.11 fewer deaths per 100,000 in small counties, compared with 9.16 statewide.
